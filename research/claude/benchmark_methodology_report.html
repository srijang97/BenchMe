Benchmark methodology for private-repo agent evaluation

Analysis of the 10 July 2026 research dossier, completed with the three literatures it missed: the RL environment-synthesis lineage, the 2026 harness-control studies, and the oracle red-teaming work. Every methodological finding mapped to an adopt / adapt / reject decision.

Prepared 4 August 2026 · Base corpus: 123-source dossier, 27 benchmark families · Added: 34 systems and studies not present in the base corpus

Executive summary

The dossier is methodologically strong and empirically incomplete

The July research is genuinely good on doctrine — the oracle assurance ladder, the five never-merge evaluation tracks, the configuration manifest as join key, the failure taxonomy with two denominators. That framework is better than anything published by a competitor. What it lacks is the 2026 empirical layer: it names techniques without yields, asserts statistical defaults without power analysis, and misses an entire parallel literature that has been solving BenchMe's hardest problem for eighteen months.

The five findings that change BenchMe's design

  1. Harness rankings are not transitive across task suites. Claw-SWE-Bench ranks OpenClaw first of five harnesses (73.4%); Harness-Bench ranks the same harness last of six (52.4%). Same names, opposite order, different task populations. This is the strongest published evidence for BenchMe's entire thesis — if harness rankings do not transfer between two public benchmarks, they certainly do not transfer to a private repo. Put this on the first slide.
  2. Harness sensitivity scales inversely with model strength. The harness spread is 10.3 pp on GLM 5.1 but 27.4 pp on the weaker Qwen 3.6-flash. Teams running cheap or open models need harness measurement 2.7× more than teams running frontier models — a real segmentation signal that also tells you which customers feel the pain first.
  3. Variance persists at temperature 0. The definitive study (60,000 trajectories, 6 configs × 500 tasks × 10 runs) finds single-run pass@1 ranges of 2.2–6.0 pp, and one configuration had higher variance at T=0 than at default. Median trajectory divergence occurs at token position 5. Determinism is not available; the dossier's "3–5 trials for close candidates" is correct but was previously unsupported.
  4. Environment reuse has a published, cheap answer, and BenchMe missed it. SWE-Factory achieves 33.5–40.1% valid instances at $0.024–0.045 each using a four-agent builder, and eliminates per-framework log parsers entirely by injecting a standardised exit-code sentinel — 100% accuracy across 2,085 test logs. SWE-Next amortises images across repo-quarters for a 48× storage reduction. Both are directly adoptable this month.
  5. Your oracle doctrine has a documented counter-example. BenchMe's rule is "execution-based primary, refuse the LLM judge." EvilGenie tested exactly this and found held-out unit tests gave only minimal improvement over other reward-hack detectors, while the LLM judge was highly effective on unambiguous cases. And an ISSTA 2026 replicability study finds coverage and mutation scores are not reliable adequacy indicators when the goal is exposing defects in buggy code. Both cut against the current plan and both need answering.

What the July research missed

Three whole literatures, plus a competitive refresh

A. The RL environment-synthesis lineage entirely absent

The dossier names SWE-smith and R2E-Gym in a single sentence and moves on. In fact this is the field that has spent two years solving "how do you construct and verify tasks at scale" — the exact problem BenchMe calls its hardest. Eight systems, all with published methodology, none in the base corpus beyond a citation.

Note the naming collision you flagged: there are two DeepSWEs. DeepSWE-Preview (Agentica + Together AI, Jul 2025) is a 32B RL-trained agent. DeepSWE the benchmark (arXiv 2607.07946, Jul 2026) is a separate 113-task, 91-repo, 5-language evaluation with hand-written implementation-agnostic verifiers. The second is the one relevant to you, and it is the most interesting oracle design published this year.

B. The 2026 harness-control studies partially present

The dossier has Harness-Bench and Claw-SWE-Bench. It does not have: the definitive variance study (2602.07150), the longitudinal 35-release scaffold study's implications, the scaffold source-code taxonomy (2604.03515), HarnessX, Meta-Harness, AgentAtlas's config-only rank inversions, or Inspect AI's agent bridge — which is a materially better way to run the model axis than the container-boundary approach BenchMe currently plans.

C. Oracle red-teaming and test-adequacy mostly absent

The dossier has UTBoost and the OpenAI audit. It lacks STING's full mutation methodology, PatchDiff's differential-testing numbers, BenchJack (automated benchmark red-teaming, 219 flaws across 10 benchmarks), SpecBench's reward-hacking gap metric, EvilGenie's detector comparison, Hack-Verifiable Environments, and the ISSTA replicability study that questions mutation score as an adequacy proxy.

The single most useful number in this report

SWE-Next's 2.2% yield, with its failure breakdown: 74.5% of candidate commit pairs show unchanged test behaviour, 20.8% fail test execution, 2.5% fail setup.

Your own pivot criterion is "<8 valid tasks per repo means stop." At a 2.2% conversion from raw commits, a repo needs roughly 360 candidate commit pairs to yield 8 capsules, and ~900 to yield 20. Any repo with 2+ years of active history clears that comfortably; a low-activity internal service does not. That gives you a mechanical qualification test you can run on a customer's repo in minutes, before any conversation about access.

It also reframes the yield problem correctly: the binding constraint is not mining sophistication, it is that three quarters of real commits simply do not move the test needle. No technique fixes that — it is a property of how people write code.

Two techniques to steal this month

SWE-Factory's exit-code sentinel. Rather than writing a log parser per test framework — which your knowledge base budgets ~1 minute per repo for, and which breaks constantly — append a command that emits EXIT_CODE=<value> and parse that. Measured at 100% accuracy against manual inspection across 2,085 test logs. This removes an entire category of adapter maintenance.

SWE-Next's repo-quarter profiles. Map commits to repo_{year}Q{quarter} and reuse one dependency environment across all commits in that window, rather than building an image per commit. 639 GB total against an estimated 30.8 TB naive. Against the dossier's own note that SWE-bench recommends 120 GB for local evaluation, this is the difference between a laptop-feasible product and a datacentre one.

The error2pass pathology new

SWE-Factory identifies a failure mode your control matrix will hit: tests that fail before the patch because of structural or collection errors rather than the bug being tested. These look like valid fail-to-pass pairs and are not. Including them deflates measured capability and inflates apparent task validity. Add an explicit check that the pre-patch failure is an assertion failure, not an import error, collection error, or syntax error.

Stage 2

Environment reconstruction

The dossier grades automatic environment setup "weak-to-moderate, low success on difficult repos" and supplies no numeric success rate anywhere. That gap matters, because the number is the whole argument for the enterprise wedge.

The published reality
  • EnvBench: best LLM-agent approach configures 6.69% of Python repos and 29.47% of JVM repos.
  • ExecutionAgent: 33/50 projects (66%), at 74 minutes and $0.16 per project.
  • SWE-Factory: environment setup succeeds on 49.8–57.2% of issues depending on builder model.
  • SWE-smith: 128 working environments from the top 5,000 PyPI packages, at ~7 minutes of human labour per repo.
Why this is your moat, not your problem

No published system reliably containerises an arbitrary repository. But your customer's CI already checks out arbitrary commits, installs dependencies and runs tests hermetically, thousands of times a day.

Environment reuse, not synthesis. This converts the field's hardest open research problem into an integration problem against Buildkite, Actions, Jenkins and Bazel — and it is the one thing a "point it at any GitHub URL" competitor structurally cannot do.

The qualification question, stated precisely

"Can you hand me a container image in which <your test command> passes at an arbitrary historical commit?"

Ranked by how easily a repo answers yes: Bazel / Nix / Pants (hermetic by construction) → devcontainers (literally a spec for this) → explicit container images in CI config → Actions with runner-native setup steps (replay setup-python, apt installs; brittle). If it takes them more than an hour, they are a services engagement, not a design partner.

The central unresolved conflict in the field

OpenAI's audit says SWE-bench's tests are too strict. STING says they are too weak. Both are correct simultaneously.

OpenAI found ≥59.4% of audited problems have flawed tests — 35.5% narrow (enforcing unspecified implementation details, rejecting functionally correct submissions) and 18.8% wide (checking behaviour the issue never disclosed). STING found 77% admit a passing wrong patch. These pull benchmark design in opposite directions and no published method satisfies both.

The best attempted resolution is the DeepSWE benchmark's design: hand-written verifiers that accept any implementation of the requested functionality, with solutions kept private. It reports 1.4% disagreement with independent evaluators against 32.4% for a competitor. That is an oracle designed for behavioural specification rather than implementation matching — and it is precisely what your fallback-salts capsule already does with its nine-point behavioural contract. You arrived at the right design independently. Say so publicly.

What "oracle strength" should actually mean on a report

Report a per-capsule profile, never a binary. Six components, each cheap to compute:

  • Mutation survival rate — % of generated wrong patches the suite fails to catch.
  • Alternate-solution acceptance — does an independently written correct implementation pass? (STING's anti-overfitting gate applies 12 behaviour-preserving transformations; steal that list.)
  • Differential divergence — PatchDiff-style, with the wrong / valid-alternative split reported separately.
  • Patch-region coverage — STING moved this 40.8% → 51.6% via augmentation.
  • Visible-hidden gap — SpecBench's reward-hacking proxy.
  • Red-team status — did a hacker agent obtain reward without completing the task?

Nobody ships this. Sigmabench's oracle is a file-level Jaccard index against a golden diff — it does not run tests at all. Stet gates on the human PR's tests and then layers LLM judgement above. Neither can report any of the six.

Stage 4

Controls, contamination and anti-gaming

The dossier's six mandatory controls are correct and better specified than any public benchmark's. The 2026 additions are about adversarial controls, which is where the field moved.

Already in the dossier — keep
  • Base negative / reference positive / regression control
  • No-op and near-miss rejection
  • Alternate-solution positive
  • Adversarial verifier probe
  • Four named information policies, never mixed in one ranking
  • git archive + single-commit re-init, remotes/tags/reflogs/alternates stripped
  • Default-deny egress with pinned offline mirror
Add from 2026
  • BenchJack's eight flaw classes as a checklist: isolation failure, leaked answers, RCE, judge prompt injection, weak string matching, evaluation-logic gaps, trusting untrusted output, excessive permissions.
  • Iterative hacker–fixer loop to convergence, not a single probe. BenchJack drove hackable-task ratio from ~100% to <10%; WebArena and OSWorld reached 0% in three iterations.
  • Hack-Verifiable planting — deliberately plant detectable exploits to verify your own detection works. "Hacking is addictive": once an agent finds an exploit it reuses it.
  • Two-container boundary, enforced. Your current setup keeps private tests "outside the candidate git root," which your own docs concede is not a boundary.

The retrieval numbers that justify sealing

Cursor's audit of successful trajectories: 57% used the public web to look up the merged fix; 9% mined bundled git history. The dossier cites a 63% overall retrieval figure and correctly warns against generalising it. Claw-SWE-Bench measured the effect of removing reachable future commits: −8.0 pp on the strongest model, and near zero on the weakest — stronger agents exploit more effectively, so sealing matters more as models improve, not less.

Non-transitivity: the finding that makes the business

Claw-SWE-Bench and Harness-Bench evaluate the same five harness families and produce opposite orderings. OpenClaw is best-of-five in one and worst-of-six in the other. The likely explanation — different task populations, plus one benchmark's adapter doing more work for one harness — is beside the point.

The point is: if a harness ranking does not transfer between two public benchmarks, no public benchmark can tell a team which harness to run on their repository. That is the product thesis, and it is now evidenced by two independent 2026 papers rather than asserted.

Inspect AI's agent bridge — a better architecture than the one you planned new

Two families of harness standardisation exist, and the dossier only considers one.

Container boundary

Harbor, HAL, Claw-SWE-Bench's five-method adapter. The agent is a black box in a container; the model is passed in via config. Clean harness-axis control — you can swap harnesses freely. But the model axis depends on each harness exposing model configuration, which closed products often do not.

API interception

Inspect AI's sandbox_agent_bridge() runs a proxy on localhost:13131 and intercepts the agent's native OpenAI/Anthropic/Google API calls, rerouting them to whichever model you choose. The harness is completely unmodified. Clean model-axis control — you can point an unmodified Claude Code at an arbitrary model.

A rigorous (model × harness) grid needs both. Interception for the model axis, container adapters for the harness axis. Epoch AI already uses inspect-swe to run Claude Code and Codex as scaffolds inside Inspect on SWE-bench Verified, so this is proven in practice. This is the highest-value architectural change in this report and it removes your dependence on each vendor exposing a model flag.

Keep mini-SWE-agent as the control arm

~100 lines of Python, bash only, does not even use the LM tool-calling interface — and it reports >74% on SWE-bench Verified. Its authors position it explicitly as a control that "puts the language model rather than the agent scaffold in the middle of our attention." Every harness grid should include it as the floor, so you can report how much of a score is scaffolding.

Read the effect-size table against the noise floor

Cross-referencing §5 against 2602.07150 disciplines the whole literature — and your own roadmap:

  • Safely real: the 54.3 pp adapter effect, the 29.4 pp model spread, the 27.4 pp and 23.8 pp harness spreads, the ~18 pp Terminal-Bench leaderboard gaps.
  • Not established: Claw-SWE-Bench's 10.3 pp harness spread on the strong model, and its −2.0 pp leakage effect on Qwen. Both are single-run and sit within or near noise.
  • Almost every harness study in this report is single-run. Harness-Bench, Claw-SWE-Bench and the Terminal-Bench leaderboard all publish point estimates. The large effects survive; the fine-grained orderings inside each study do not.

Practical consequence for your product: a config-regression suite is a guardrail, not an optimiser. You can reliably detect a harness swap or a model change. You cannot reliably detect whether a prompt tweak helped by 2 pp without 9 repeats per task, which no customer will pay for weekly. Sell it as "did we break anything," not "did we improve."

HAL's honesty, worth copying

The Holistic Agent Leaderboard (ICLR 2026) is the closest thing to BenchMe's intended reporting — explicitly three-dimensional (models × scaffolds × benchmarks), 142 combinations, 21,730 rollouts, ~$40,000, with cost-vs-accuracy Pareto frontiers and a frozen price table. It also states plainly:

"At these prices, running multiple trials to construct confidence intervals becomes prohibitively expensive. For HAL, we were forced to rely on single runs without statistical validation for most evaluations."

Its motivating statistic — "agents can be 100× more expensive while only being 1% better" — plus the concrete case of a scaffold/model pair costing 9× more for a 2 pp difference, is the best available third-party support for cost-per-solved-task as a primary metric.

Stage 7

Governance and versioning

The dossier is genuinely excellent here — task lifecycle states, the correction-creates-a-version rule, the nine technical kill switches, the 14-item minimum reporting standard. Two additions.

Benchmark repair moves scores more than model upgrades

Terminal-Bench 2.1 patched 28 of 89 tasks across three defect classes — external dependency drift (9), resource/timeout mismatches (8), and instruction–verifier misalignment. Effect on identical agent+model pairs: Claude Opus 4.6 + Claude Code went 58.0 → 70.1 (+12.1 pp); Gemini 3 Flash +9.4; GPT-5.4 mini +8.3.

A benchmark hygiene patch moved scores further than most model releases do. This is the single best argument for your recurring-revenue story: a task corpus is not a static asset, it decays, and someone has to maintain it. Nobody re-derives their internal 105-case suite quarterly.

The dossier's own best line, worth keeping verbatim

"'Human verified' is a process description, not a permanent validity guarantee. Benchmark validity is conditional on the systems being evaluated — stronger systems expose latent task defects, so a recurring task-audit loop is required, not a one-time validation badge."

That is the subscription argument in two sentences. It is better than anything in the strategy documents.

Synthesis

Concrete design decisions

Adopt now — cheap, high-value, this month

  1. Two-container grading boundary. Agent runs in container A with no test access; extract only the patch; apply and grade in fresh container B. Your current "outside the git root" is not a boundary and your own docs say so. Smallest change with the largest correctness gain.
  2. Mutation hardening over fallback-salts. STING's method: 32 operator-based mutation rules across 7 categories, plus LLM semantic mutants, then the 12 behaviour-preserving transformations as an anti-overfitting gate. Produces your first oracle-strength number and turns the v1 finding from anecdote into metric.
  3. Exit-code sentinel instead of log parsers. SWE-Factory's trick. 100% accuracy over 2,085 logs. Deletes a whole class of adapter maintenance.
  4. error2pass check. Assert the pre-patch failure is an assertion failure, not an import/collection/syntax error.
  5. Instrument cache hit rate. Both Codex and Claude Code report cached input tokens in their JSONL traces. One afternoon; replaces the biggest assumption in your cost model.

Adopt at design-partner stage

  • Inspect AI agent bridge for the model axis, container adapters for the harness axis. Removes your dependence on vendors exposing model flags.
  • Repo-quarter environment profiles — 48× storage reduction, and it makes local-first credible on a laptop.
  • mini-SWE-agent as the permanent control arm in every harness grid.
  • BenchJack's eight flaw classes as a red-team checklist, run iteratively to convergence rather than once.
  • SpecBench's visible-minus-hidden gap as a standing reward-hacking metric.
  • The 2.2%-yield qualification test — count candidate commit pairs in a customer's repo before asking for access.

Reject, or defer explicitly

  • Learned surrogate environments (SWE-World). Radical and interesting, but its fidelity against real Docker execution is unpublished. A reward model that can be gamed is a reward-hacking surface. Not for a product whose entire claim is verification.
  • Bug injection for the decision set. SWE-smith is cheap ($2.32/1,000) and useful for volume, but DeepSWE reported it gave "limited improvement" for RL and SWE-Playground reports poor out-of-domain transfer. Synthetic bugs are not your customer's bugs.
  • Generated-from-scratch projects. No customer relevance.
  • Mutation score as a headline product metric — see the conflict below.

Open conflicts

Four things this research does not resolve

1. Mutation score may not measure what you want it to

The ISSTA 2026 replicability study (arXiv 2607.22880) finds coverage and mutation scores give meaningful cross-model signal in the regression setting — where code is assumed correct — but are not reliable indicators when the goal is exposing defects in buggy code.

Your use case sits in between: you use mutants to test whether a suite can distinguish a correct patch from a wrong one, which is closer to regression than defect-detection. STING's own results (329 patches killed across 10 leading agents) suggest it works in practice. But you should validate it on your own capsules before making it the headline number, and report mutation survival alongside alternate-solution acceptance rather than alone.

2. Your "refuse the LLM judge" doctrine has a counter-example

EvilGenie compared three reward-hack detectors and found held-out unit tests gave only minimal improvement over the alternatives, while an LLM judge was highly effective on unambiguous cases. Meanwhile Stet's Equivalence metric — which is an LLM judge — surfaces 50 pp gaps between models with identical pass rates, i.e. it discriminates where the execution gate cannot.

The defensible position is narrower than "refuse the judge": execution is primary for correctness; a grounded, calibrated judge is primary for the things execution cannot see — scope, compatibility, maintainability, and whether a passing patch is a hack. Factory's report-grounding technique (variance 7% → 0.6%) is the mechanism for making judged metrics stable. Reposition rather than defend.

3. Strict versus weak tests cannot both be fixed by the same method

OpenAI's 35.5%-narrow finding and STING's 77%-weak finding push in opposite directions. Hardening tests to kill mutants makes them stricter, which increases the false-negative rate against valid alternative implementations — and PatchDiff found 46.8% of behavioural divergences were legitimate alternatives. Hardening without an alternate-solution control will manufacture the exact defect OpenAI deprecated SWE-bench for.

Mitigation: never harden without running the alternate-solution positive control afterwards. The DeepSWE benchmark's implementation-agnostic verifier design is the current best answer and matches what your fallback-salts capsule already does.

4. The unpublished numbers that matter most

  • R2E-Gym's stage-by-stage yield and pipeline cost — genuinely not published, and its Docker build scripts still rely on semi-manual dependency-pin searching. That is the un-automated bottleneck the whole lineage works around.
  • SWE-World's fidelity against real execution — the single number that would justify or kill learned environments.
  • SWE-Hub (Baidu) publishes a full production architecture with zero yields, task counts or costs.
  • No vendor in this space publishes list pricing. Sigmabench, Stet, RepoGauge, Factory, Vals AI, Agent CI — all demo-gated. You have no pricing anchor from competitors, only from adjacent categories.

The July dossier built the right doctrine and missed the evidence. Everything it proposed on oracles, tracks, manifests and governance survives contact with the 2026 literature; what changes is that you now have published yields, a real power analysis, two better architectural primitives, and — in harness non-transitivity — a piece of third-party evidence for the core thesis that is stronger than anything you had before.
